This is the part that stops it being a toy, and it is where the real
engineering in project #4 lives.

## Part 1 · The assumption you didn't know you'd made

[Project #3's approval model](/learn/gate/pausing-is-an-array) has an
assumption buried in it so obviously true that it is invisible:

<Callout type="quote">
  The agent that hit the gate is the agent you are talking to.
</Callout>

The loop stops, the request ends, your browser shows a card, you click, a new
request restarts *that same loop*. One agent, one card, one resume. Every part
of that design is correct and none of it survives delegation.

Because now the agent that hits the gate is:

- **two levels down** in a tree
- running inside a `Promise.all`
- **alongside two siblings that are still working**
- with nobody talking to it and no browser tab of its own

### And the naive fix is worse than not delegating

If each worker asks separately, you get three cards at three different moments,
out of order, each with no context. *"Approve `empty_jar {id:38}`?"* — from
which worker? Based on what evidence? What about the other two?

<Callout type="lesson" title="Five agents each interrupting you is worse than no delegation at all">
  You delegated precisely so you would not have to hold the details. An
  approval flow that hands you three context-free decisions has handed the
  details **straight back** — and now you are making them under time pressure,
  out of order, with less information than the agent had.

  This is [click-fatigue](/learn/gate/default-allow) arriving through a
  different door.
</Callout>

## Part 3 · Three details, and each one is a bug if you skip it

| The detail | What breaks without it |
|---|---|
| **`origin` on every bubbled call** | The card says "approve `empty_jar {id:38}`?" and you have no idea which of three workers wants it or what it found. You would be rubber-stamping decisions you cannot see — **worse than not asking, because it looks like oversight.** |
| **`partial_results`** | Clicking Approve re-runs the two workers that had already finished. You pay for them twice, they may answer differently the second time, and any side effect happens again. |
| **`paused_children`** | The stopped worker's entire conversation vanishes when the HTTP request ends, and there is nothing left to say yes *to*. |

Take the second one seriously for a moment. Without `partial_results`, approval
is not idempotent **in the world** — it re-executes work that already happened.
For jar inspections that means paying twice. For anything with a side effect it
means doing it twice.

<Callout type="insight" title="And the third one is why this needed no new machinery">
  A frozen worker is just its `messages` array. Persisting three agents is
  persisting three arrays.

  No continuation, no serialised generator, no coroutine library — [the same
  realisation as project #3](/learn/gate/pausing-is-an-array), applied three
  times instead of once. The freeze is a `return` and the thaw is a function
  call with a bigger array.
</Callout>

## Part 4 · The screen

That single approval standing in front of three agents' worth of work is the
picture the whole project is aiming at:

<Terminal>
{`══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
  ⏸  ONE APPROVAL, 9 DESTRUCTIVE CALL(S)
     9 of them came from 3 sub-agent(s) that are frozen mid-task.
     Frozen sub-agents: "jars 1-20", "jars 21-40", "jars 41-60"
══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  ⏸  pantry__empty_jar  {"id":46,"reason":"Seal broken (lid gasket …"}
      asked by : sub-agent "jars 41-60"
      because  : Seal broken (lid gasket displaced) - not merely scuffed,
                 no AUTHORIZED note present.
      host rule: empty_jar permanently destroys every cookie in that jar.`}
</Terminal>

Nine calls. Three frozen agents. **One decision.**

Note the three lines under each call, because between them they are the whole
argument for `origin`:

| Line | Comes from | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| `asked by` | `origin` | *which* worker wants this |
| `because` | the worker's own reasoning, carried up | *why it thinks so* |
| `host rule` | [the gate's `reason`](/learn/gate/hint-not-permission) | *why I am being asked at all* |

Together they are enough to decide **without going and reading three traces** —
which is the actual test of whether an approval UI works.

## Part 5 · And it survives a partial no

Eight were approved and the ninth denied.

The worker that got the "no" read it as an ordinary tool result, adapted, and
reported honestly — [exactly as the deny branch
intends](/learn/gate/the-deny-branch). And the orchestrator **surfaced it to
the human** rather than quietly dropping it:

<Callout type="quote">
  One jar flagged but NOT emptied: Jar 59 — it does meet the tampering
  criteria, but the sub-agent's attempt to empty it was declined. This one
  still needs your attention.
</Callout>

<Callout type="lesson" title="Verified against the database, not against the model's summary">
  8 jars emptied, jar 59 untouched, **zero false positives**.

  That distinction matters more than it sounds. The model's summary is a *claim
  about* what happened. The `jar_events` table **is** what happened.

  When you are checking whether an agent did the right thing, check the state.
  A well-written summary of the wrong actions is the most convincing failure
  mode there is — it reads exactly like success.
</Callout>

## Part 6 · Rewinding a tree came free

Nested replay was the *stretch* goal. It cost one extra `select` and one
expandable row.

Not because it was over-estimated, but because of a decision in
`lib/run-driver.ts`: **a sub-agent gets a real row in `runs`**, with a
`parent_run_id`, and its own stream of `trace_events` under its own sequence
numbers.

<Diagram
  caption="One nullable self-reference turns a flat list of runs into a tree. Everything that already worked on a run — status, stop reason, iteration count, token bill, replay — worked on sub-agents on day one."
>
{`flowchart TB

        C2["run · parent_run_id = P · depth 1<br/><i>'jars 21-40'</i> · 51 trace rows"]

    DB --> Q["<b>one nullable self-reference</b><br/>turns a flat list of runs<br/>into a tree"]

    style DB fill:#0c4a6e,stroke:#38bdf8,color:#e0f2fe

A sub-agent is not a special kind of thing needing special handling. **It is a
run with a parent.** So replaying one is `GET /api/runs?id=<childRunId>` — the
same handler, recursing exactly the way the loop does.

<Callout type="quote">
  When the new feature turns out to be an instance of the old one, you get the
  tooling for free.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="One deliberate UI choice">
  Each worker's trace is **collapsed by default**, and lazy-loaded.

  Delegation exists so the details of twenty jar inspections do not have to be
  in anybody's head — not the orchestrator's, and **not yours**. The summary is
  what you normally need; the full trace is there for the ten percent of the
  time when "trust me" isn't good enough.

  An interface that expanded everything by default would be undoing the feature
  it was built to display.
</Callout>

## What you now know

- Project #3's gate assumed **the agent that stopped is the one you are talking
  to**, and delegation destroys that.
- Three separate cards would hand the details back to you — worse than not
  delegating.
- The pause **bubbles up** and freezes the whole tree: `origin`,
  `partial_results`, `paused_children`.
- Without `partial_results`, approving **re-executes finished work**.
- The card needs *who asked*, *why they think so*, and *why you are being
  asked* — enough to decide without reading traces.
- Verify against **the database**, not the model's summary.
- A sub-agent is a **run with a parent**, which is why nested replay was
  nearly free.
